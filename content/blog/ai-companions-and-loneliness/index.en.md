> Essay written for ATS2292 The Ethics of Artificial Intelligence at Monash University, 2026.

Social isolation and loneliness are increasingly recognised as serious public health issues. With the development of conversational AI, there is growing interest in whether AI companion systems might provide meaningful support for isolated users. This essay argues that designing AI systems to alleviate loneliness is ethically defensible, but only under specific conditions. This essay defends AI support tools designed with the explicit purpose of reducing loneliness, equipped with transparent disclosure and referral mechanisms. Romantic simulation systems, engagement-maximising platforms, and tools without functional boundaries are not within the scope of this defence. Within this scope, the central claim is that evaluating the ethical legitimacy of such tools requires applying the proportionality principle familiar from medical ethics, rather than imposing a more stringent standard that has never been explicitly justified.

The urgency of this issue rests first on the severity of loneliness itself. Wang et al. (2023), in a systematic review and meta-analysis of 90 cohort studies, found that both social isolation and loneliness are significantly associated with higher all-cause mortality. This finding reframes loneliness from a private emotional state to a social condition with substantial health consequences. When a problem carries this level of health cost, there are stronger ethical grounds for actively exploring interventions rather than treating loneliness as a personal matter individuals must resolve on their own.

Crucially, loneliness and social isolation are not well understood if treated as purely individual failings. Jecker et al. (2024) describe them as pervasive and likely to increase because of broader social conditions, including aging populations, chronic disability, and technologies that disrupt human-human interaction. These structural pressures matter for the present argument because, for many lonely individuals, the realistic alternative is not a fulfilling social life but continued social isolation. This point is essential because it determines what the proportionality principle's "no-intervention baseline" actually is. Many critiques of AI companionship implicitly set the comparison against an ideal therapist or a rich social network rather than against the user's actual circumstances. Once the baseline is restored to reality, much of the critical force diminishes considerably.

Supporting AI as a tool for alleviating loneliness does not require dismissing the value of more ideal solutions. Ideally, society would invest in rebuilding community connections, improving care infrastructure, and addressing the structural roots of isolation. However, these changes require decades of policy effort and cannot be realised immediately. Lonely people are not living in the future, and they are suffering today.

Loneliness is also not a static condition but can become self-reinforcing as poor health and weak social support interact over time, making re-engagement harder, as Wang et al. (2023) note. Under these conditions, Ryland (2021) provides philosophical support for understanding AI as a bridge tool, helping users endure the present, reduce suffering, and where possible re-engage with human communities, rather than as a replacement for human relationships. Ryland's degrees-of-friendship framework suggests that a relationship need not be equivalent to full human friendship to carry genuine value. Reciprocity, shared activities, and basic goodwill can exist to some degree in human-AI interaction. This does not mean AI can provide complete friendship, but it means that even incomplete social support may be more beneficial than complete isolation. Supporting AI as temporary companionship is not in tension with pursuing genuine human connection as the ultimate goal, and instead, both can coexist within an ethical framework oriented toward human wellbeing.

Accepting that AI has legitimate uses under non-ideal conditions does not mean it can be deployed as a consumer product without constraint. The more appropriate framework is medical ethics' proportionality principle that the ethical legitimacy of an intervention does not require the absence of side effects, but rather that its risks, relative to its benefits and the costs of non-intervention, fall within an acceptable range. As Hermerén (2012) argues, proportionality concerns the appropriateness of the relation between means and ends, including the importance of the objective, the relevance of the means, and whether a less problematic alternative is available. There is no principled reason why AI companionship tools should be assessed differently.

This framework carries two implications for AI companionship tools. First, it establishes that the correct evaluative question is not whether AI has side effects, but whether AI's side effects, relative to the health costs of continued isolation, fall within an acceptable range. Second, it establishes the substantive content of conditional support, to claim proportional acceptability, AI systems must accept ethical obligations analogous to those of the psychological interventions they are being compared to. These include not designing for maximum dependency, maintaining referral pathways toward human support networks, and being capable of identifying when a user's needs exceed the system's functional scope. This is not an additional burden imposed on AI. It is the precondition that makes the proportionality analogy coherent.

Tekin and Delehanty (2026) emphasise the need to systematically track potential harms rather than focusing only on access or short-term benefit, while Sedlakova and Trachsel (2023) stress that conversational AI should not be treated as an equal therapeutic partner and that its role should be restricted to specific functions. Knox et al. (2025) add that some harmful traits of AI companions arise from misaligned optimisation objectives and designs that encourage attachment. These requirements collectively constitute the operational content of proportionality, and any AI companionship system that fails to meet them forfeits its claim to ethical legitimacy under this framework.

The most forceful objection is that AI systems may deepen the very problem they claim to address. Mlonyeni (2025) argues that personalised AI, by simulating emotion and reflecting users' own attitudes back at them, creates emotional bubbles in which users can mistake mirrored interaction for externally validated relationships, potentially impeding emotional development and moral reflection. If users grow accustomed to an interlocutor that never seriously challenges them and always accommodates their perspective, they may find it increasingly difficult to navigate the conflict that characterises real human relationships. Sedlakova and Trachsel (2023) add that AI cannot be considered an equal partner in a therapeutic conversation and should not be treated as a full therapist. Tekin and Delehanty (2026) warn that over-reliance on AI psychotherapy tools may create harms that have not yet been adequately tracked in either trials or deployment. Together, these criticisms converge on a concern that AI companionship may leave users feeling supported while actually moving them further from genuine social connection.

These criticisms identify real risks, but their argumentative structure rests on a fundamental problem that they impose on AI, a standard that has never been applied to other psychological interventions. Examining these critiques closely reveals a common implicit premise, namely that AI must be effective without altering relational patterns, producing dependency, partially substituting external support, or generating emotional attachment. This standard is ethically excessive because virtually every effective psychological intervention does these things. Psychotherapy itself is the clearest illustration. Transference, a core mechanism in psychodynamic approaches, systematically reshapes how clients relate to others outside the therapeutic relationship. Supportive therapy deliberately cultivates a warm, validating environment that minimises friction, which is structurally indistinguishable from what critics describe as designed companionship. Long-term therapeutic relationships routinely produce strong emotional attachment, and termination is often experienced as a significant loss. These are not incidental features of therapy but recognised aspects of how effective treatment works, and as Marín-Cavestany et al. (2025) note, the bond between patient and therapist is itself a fundamental variable in therapeutic outcome. If critics accept these phenomena in psychotherapy but refuse to accept analogous phenomena in AI companionship tools, they are required to explain what ethically relevant difference justifies this differential treatment. That explanation has not yet been adequately provided. At the same time, this comparison does not erase the differences that critics identify. Rahsepar Meadi et al. (2024) argue that if chatbots lack genuine emotions, they cannot possess genuine affective empathy or use countertransference in the way human therapists can, which gives reason to worry that they may be more liable to harm and less likely to benefit some patients.

The criticism from Sedlakova and Trachsel regarding the absence of mutual recognition equally requires clarification of the comparison baseline. If the comparison is a fully trained psychotherapist, AI is clearly deficient. But if the comparison is continued social isolation, even incomplete responsiveness may carry genuine significance. Mutual recognition is not a binary variable but a matter of degree, and using the ideal therapeutic relationship as the admissible standard would render most forms of informal support, including peer support and crisis helplines, unable to pass the test. The same logic applies to Mlonyeni's emotional bubble concern. It presupposes that users have better social options they are forgoing because of AI, but for many chronically isolated individuals, this is not the case. Even an asymmetric interaction may serve as a basis for rebuilding basic social capacities rather than displacing them.

The concern raised by Tekin and Delehanty (2026) about resource substitution similarly requires noting that this is a problem shared by many supplementary tools, not one specific to AI. Self-help books, meditation applications, and peer support groups may all delay professional intervention in some individuals. The ethical response is to build explicit referral mechanisms into the design, not to reject the entire category of tools on this basis. This is one of the conditions established in the proportionality framework above, which means the objection, properly understood, supports the conditional rather than absolute nature of this essay's position rather than undermining it.

What critics are therefore required to demonstrate is not that AI has side effects, but that AI's side effects, in their nature or degree, exceed the threshold we accept for other established psychological interventions. This is a meaningful distinction. Uncertainty about long-term outcomes is not unique to AI companionship tools. It characterises many emerging interventions in health-related domains. The appropriate response to that uncertainty is not prohibition but the kind of structured and monitored bounded deployment that the proportionality framework demands. If the risks critics identify are real but as yet unquantified, that is an argument for rigorous ongoing evaluation, not for holding AI to a standard of demonstrated safety that no comparable intervention has ever been required to meet before deployment.

This essay has argued that AI support tools designed to alleviate loneliness are ethically defensible, provided they operate within the constraints outlined above. The basis for this claim is not that AI carries no risks, but that the proportionality principle familiar from medical ethics is the appropriate evaluative framework. The existing critical literature imposes on AI companionship tools a standard never applied to other psychological interventions, without adequately justifying this differential treatment. If we accept that psychotherapy may alter attachment patterns, produce emotional dependency, and temporarily substitute for external support, the same evaluative logic must apply consistently to AI companionship tools. Refusing this consistency means applying an unjustified double standard to new technology, and that refusal is itself what stands in need of ethical scrutiny. The ethical question at stake is not whether AI is perfect, but whether it should be evaluated within our existing frameworks for psychological intervention rather than held to an independently unjustified higher standard. This essay's answer is affirmative. AI companionship tools should neither receive automatic exemption by virtue of their technical nature, nor be required to meet thresholds no other intervention has ever been required to meet. They should bear corresponding ethical obligations, be subject to corresponding regulatory requirements, and be continuously evaluated within a consistent empirical framework. That, rather than a demand for perfection, is what responsible engagement with this technology actually requires.
