> Research essay for ATS1254 Culture, Power and Difference at Monash University, 2026.

*Essay question: Indigenous people Australia wide are working hard to preserve their languages. Why is this important and what are some of the key issues associated with documenting Indigenous languages in the 21st century?*

Language preservation is important to the contemporary Australian indigenous community. It is not as a heritage conservation, and instead, it is about maintenance of an entire way of being that connects people to Country, ancestors, community, and knowledge systems that cannot be translated to English without loss (Bradley, 2017). As a student from Taiwan, which is largely shaped by multiple colonial histories, I come to this place as an outsider to indigenous communities, but growing up where different languages live in daily life taught me that what language carries often exceeds what is visible on the surface. However, language preservation and documentation are not the same thing. As Himmelmann (1998) demonstrates, the purpose of documentation is to create a representative record of a language. This essay argues that language documentation in the 21st century faces a fundamental tension that the act of recording risks transforming a living language into a static one, replicating the colonial structures of knowledge extraction even as it attempts to preserve, This essay first examines why indigenous language preservation is important as a practice of cultural continuity, epistemological diversity and community wellbeing. Then this essay will turn into three issues in documentation, including the politics about who controls the record, the losses inherent in transformation, and the risk of freezing language into an authoritative form.

## Why preservation matters

Language is the primary connection between people, Country, ancestors, non-human entities, kinship, and knowledge practices. It is also the medium through which those relationships are evoked and sustained (Bradley, 2017). When the question “Can my Country hear English?” (Bradley, 2017) is asked, it expresses more than just the grief over linguistic change. It highlights that Country is the living domain that can only be perceived and responded to through specific linguistic relationships. Ringel (2022) argues that the disappearance of a language weakens the connections between people, land, and ancestors, which are far beyond the loss of speech. Speaking a language involves more than using a set of vocabulary. It is also an entry into a specific relationship with the world. Therefore, the preservation of indigenous languages is more than simple heritage conservation to encompass the maintenance of an entire way of being.

The stakes of language loss extend beyond any single community’s relationships with Country. Every language represents a unique conceptual pathway of understanding the world that exists nowhere else. Mainstream perspectives often assume that languages are merely different codes and that as long as meaning is translatable, language loss is not critical. However, Evans (2009) uses Kayardild to demonstrate how certain Australian Indigenous languages challenge linguistic assumptions of universal grammar, for instance, through nouns that mark tense, reflecting fundamentally different ways of understanding time, events, and relationships. The disappearance of a language involves the loss of an irreplaceable logic of knowledge which is not the content that can be seamlessly transplanted into English. Bracknell (2020) further emphasizes that these conceptual pathways reside not only within abstract grammar but also within song traditions, performance forms, and shared community knowledge practices. Therefore, the significance of preserving Indigenous languages lies in safeguarding epistemological possibilities that are untranslatable and cannot be equated in other languages. Precisely because these pathways are irreplaceable, the challenge is not how to freeze language in time, but how to ensure they continue to flow.

These irreplaceable epistemological possibilities are not abstract concerns. Language loss is consistently linked to concrete harm to community health and wellbeing, which are far beyond being an abstract cultural symbol. Language loss is often entwined with land dispossession, assimilation policies, family displacement, and cultural shame, and consequently, language revitalization functions as a form of holistic healing rather than just linguistic recovery. Sivak et al. (2019) in their study on Barngarla language reclamation, show how participants reconnect language revitalization with spirituality, ancestors, Country, family, community, identity, and pride. Language is a component of community wellbeing itself, and it is not something re-attached after cultural damage has occurred. A realist review by Whalen et al. (2022) further demonstrates that language use and revitalization are consistently linked to stronger socio-emotional support. A global scoping review by Harding et al. (2025) identifies positive correlations between Indigenous languages and mental health, resilience, and reduction of substance use. Language preservation is therefore an active practice addressing the contemporary wellbeing and flourishing of communities and the continuity of future generations.

## Who controls the record

If a language is irreplaceable, as it is a mode of existence, a knowledge system, and a fundamental part of being, the question of how it is documented becomes as urgent as whether it is documented. The act of recording is never neutral. It involves questions of who decides what is worth preserving, who organizes the material, who maintains the access to this information, and who ultimately holds the power of interpretation. Bradley (2017) observes that the transformation of oral knowledge into academic information often results in it being decontextualized from its local and relational origins. It becomes categorized, managed, and citable data, a process that risks replicating colonial knowledge structures. This issue can also be seen more clearly within the framework of Traditional Ecological Knowledge (TEK). Bradley argues that when indigenous knowledge is defined as systematizable knowledge, although it seems to be recognized, it is often extracted from its original lived context, becoming a knowledge object that western institutions can identify, organize, and manage. In other words, TEK does not provide a neutral classification name. Rather, it may repackage the language that originally existed as everyday knowledge into categories within external academic and governance systems. Thus, the core issue of language documentation should be beyond whether content is preserved to whether the community retains its agency as the primary subject of knowledge.

For documentation to become genuinely part of language maintenance rather than extraction, it must be built on community-led governance rather than external research frameworks. This is demonstrated by the case of Kununurra in Western Australia, where the local community proactively initiated the recording of Miriwoong sign language, decided on its name, and successfully applied for the international ISO 639-3 code (Adone & Gosebrink, 2025). Sebastian and Giovanangeli (2023) similarly note that even within policies supporting language revival, state institutions may use educational and legal frameworks to define legitimate language practices. Ultimately, documentation is never a simple matter of preservation, but it is a matter of power.

## What is lost in transcription

A second issue lies in what is lost in the shift from oral practice to text or digital archive. While language documentation is frequently conceptualized as preservation, this perspective overlooks the inherent losses caused by the shift in medium. In highly oral traditions, meaning resides not only in lexical content but also in the context of speech, including tone, rhythm, embodied practice, listener relationships, and the land itself. Bradley (2017), reflecting on Yanyuwa oral traditions and print representation, notes that once a story is transformed into a readable and analyzable text, it loses the vitality and community memory functions it possessed in the moment of telling. Bracknell (2020) further argues that language is inseparable from song and performance, and the completion of dictionaries, transcripts, or corpora does not guarantee the actual preservation of knowledge practices.  This is illustrated in Ringel’s (2022) case study of Miriwoong. Her study points out that research and teaching are more effective when conducted on Country than in an office environment because elders are more likely to recall vocabulary and cultural memory when they are in familiar places and can better connect language and knowledge through the place. In this case, language is remembered not only because the words are preserved, but also because people are still in the relationships and environments that evoke language. The Miriwoong example therefore suggests that if documentation only incorporates language into teaching materials and databases without preserving its actual connection to Country, it may preserve the linguistics form while weakening the context that vitalizes the language.

## The risk of freezing a living language

Documentation frequently freezes a language into a certain authentic form, ignoring the reality that living languages naturally evolve, which is a critical issue in documentation. Within the contexts of language revival, communities, schools, and policy institutions often want to identify the purest version of a language for transmission. While such approaches may facilitate teaching and institutional support, they also risk transforming language form a living practice into a static artifact in the museum for reproduction and display. Research (Sebastian & Giovanangeli, 2023) indicates that when indigenous languages are integrated into institutional education, they are often assumed to be standardizable. Significant differences exist between different language groups and even closely related dialects that cannot be interchangeably used. The study mentioned a Bundjalung Elder who recalled a non-Indigenous teacher sourcing the word for “echidna” from the Githabul dialect online for local classes. The Elder responded with “That’s not my language,” emphasizing that it was not the specific Bandjalung form belonging to her community. This example demonstrates that when external data is treated as an authoritative version, language is reduced to interchangeable, replicable content, and the actual relationship between the community and the language is erased.

If the content and format of language teaching are not decided collaboratively with the local community but by external institutions defining what constitutes the correct version, then revitalization may become another from of constraint. Research on Queensland First Nations language programs (O’Brien & Bobongie-Harris, 2023) thus demonstrates the emphasis on co-design and cultural inclusivity. The risk of documentation is not only insufficient for preservation. It is the potential to reshape a living and locally varying language into a stable but ultimately detached form. Language change does not necessarily signify decline, and often, change is the evidence that the language is still alive and in use in the contemporary world.

## Conclusion

Language is not just a way of describing a worldview. The importance of language preservation extends beyond cultural heritage. It is the continuity of ways of being, knowing and belonging to Country. As this essay has argued, documentation cannot guarantee that continuity. When the recording replicates the colonial structure of knowledge extraction, takes the language away from the oral and place-related context, or freezes the practices into an authorized version, it could result in the preservation in name only. The question is no longer simply why indigenous people are working to preserve the languages. Instead, the harder question is whether the tools and institutions built to support that work are truly serving the communities they claim to help.
